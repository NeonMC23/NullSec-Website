Chat Control: The EU's Assault on Encryption

July 27, 202616 min read
Quick Summary

The European Commission's Chat Control proposal would require messaging platforms to scan all user communications for illegal content — effectively breaking end-to-end encryption. Despite massive opposition from privacy advocates, tech companies, and EU member states, the proposal continues to evolve through the legislative process. This article explains the technical mechanisms (client-side scanning, message filtering), the political landscape, and what you can do to oppose it.

#ClientSideScanning #E2EE #Censorship

In May 2022, the European Commission published a proposal for a regulation that would fundamentally change the way private communications are handled in the European Union. Officially titled the "Regulation to prevent and combat child sexual abuse," it is better known as Chat Control — and it represents one of the most significant threats to encrypted communications in the democratic world.

What Chat Control Proposes

The regulation would require messaging platforms, email providers, and social media companies to scan all user communications — including those protected by end-to-end encryption — for child sexual abuse material (CSAM). The scanning would happen proactively, meaning platforms would be legally obligated to examine every message, photo, and video shared by their users.

The proposal has gone through several iterations since 2022. The original version mandated "client-side scanning" — software running on the user's device that would analyse content before it was encrypted and sent. After widespread criticism, the Commission revised the proposal to require "detection orders" that would apply only to platforms deemed "at risk." However, the fundamental tension remains: any system that scans communications undermines the security guarantees of encryption.

"Client-side scanning is not a middle ground. It is a fundamental break with the principle of secure communication." — Matthew Green, cryptographer and professor at Johns Hopkins University

The Technical Problem

The core issue is mathematical. End-to-end encryption works by ensuring that only the sender and recipient possess the keys needed to decrypt a message. Any system that can scan messages on the device before encryption — or on the server after decryption — introduces a third party that can access the content.

Proponents of Chat Control argue that "hash-matching" technology can detect known CSAM without breaking encryption. The idea is that communications are compared against a database of hashes (digital fingerprints) of known illegal images. However, this approach has fundamental flaws:

  • Hash-based detection can be bypassed by simply altering the image slightly, changing its hash.
  • It can be expanded to detect any type of content — hate speech, disinformation, political dissent — by adding new hashes to the database. Once the infrastructure exists, scope creep is inevitable.
  • False positives are unavoidable, leading to innocent communications being flagged and reviewed.
  • It breaks forward secrecy, one of the key security guarantees of modern encryption protocols.

The Political Battle

The Chat Control proposal has become one of the most contested pieces of EU legislation in recent history. The European Parliament has taken a strong stance in favour of encryption, voting repeatedly to exclude end-to-end encrypted communications from scanning requirements. However, the Council of the EU — representing member state governments — has been more divided.

Civil society organisations including European Digital Rights (EDRi), the Electronic Frontier Foundation, and the Signal Foundation have mounted a sustained campaign against the proposal. In a rare show of unity, major technology companies — including Apple, Meta, and Signal — have all opposed client-side scanning.

Several EU member states, including Germany, the Netherlands, and Austria, have expressed serious reservations about the impact on privacy and security. Other states, led by France and Poland, have pushed for stronger scanning requirements.

What's at Stake

If Chat Control becomes law, the consequences would be global. The EU's regulatory power (the "Brussels effect") means that platforms would implement these changes worldwide, not just in Europe. The result would be the systematic weakening of end-to-end encryption across the internet.

Privacy advocates argue that there is no trade-off to be made: strong encryption protects everyone, including children. Weakening encryption to detect CSAM would also weaken it against surveillance by malicious actors, hostile governments, and hackers.

How to Oppose It

  • Contact your MEP and national government representatives
  • Support organisations like EDRi and the EFF
  • Use and promote end-to-end encrypted services like Signal
  • Raise awareness about the technical realities of client-side scanning
  • Follow the legislative process at edri.org
